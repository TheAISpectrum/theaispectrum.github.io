The word *cloud* makes computing sound weightless. But the systems that train and run AI occupy buildings, use electricity, and depend on manufactured hardware.

Looking at that physical foundation changes the question. Alongside “What can this model do?”, we should ask: **what does it take to make it work?**

## Follow the electricity

The International Energy Agency’s 2025 report *Energy and AI* examines the electricity needed by data centres and how AI is changing demand. It is a useful starting point, with one important distinction: data centres support many digital services, so their total electricity use is not all attributable to AI.

Training a model is one part of its energy story. Running it to produce answers is another. To understand an environmental claim, first check which activity is being measured.

## Look beyond a single number

Electricity is only one part of the footprint. Cooling, the manufacture of computing equipment, and the eventual replacement of that equipment also belong in the discussion. Their impacts depend on where and how a system is built and operated.

A universal “cost per prompt” can hide these differences. Before repeating such a figure, ask:

- Which model and task does it describe?
- Does it cover training, everyday use, or both?
- What electricity supply and cooling system are assumed?
- What is excluded from the estimate?

> A useful answer about AI’s footprint needs a boundary: what, exactly, are we counting?

## A more useful conversation

Our starting position is that environmental costs deserve to be visible. That means asking for clear measurements and comparing the value of an application with the resources it requires.

“Digital” is a description of how a service works. It is not a promise that the service has no physical cost.
