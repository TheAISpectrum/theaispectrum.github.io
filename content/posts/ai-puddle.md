Every time someone types a question into an Artificial intelligence agent like ChatGPT or Gemini , something invisible happens hundreds and even thousands of miles away. Somewhere in a windowless building the size of several football fields, thousands of servers work non-stop to answer your questions, while generating heat. These servers need to be cooled to avoid overheating of these systems. Often these systems are cleaned with water, like the water which comes out of your kitchen tap. This may sound like science fiction, but it isn't: **According to arXiv preprint, training GPT-3 in Microsoft's U.S. data centers could consume a total of 5.4 million liters of water, including 700,000 liters of on-site water consumption for cooling alone.** If we multiply that by every AI model being trained and run today, a pattern emerges. As AI becomes integrated into anything and everything from search engines to customer service bots, the environmental price tag behind the scenes is growing just as fast as the technology itself.
AI data centers do have some good sides. They can help power grids run better, catch equipment problems early, and make shipping and logistics smoother — often saving more than they use up. One big, well-built data center also tends to use less energy per task than lots of small, messy ones doing the same job. All the attention on their water and power use is also pushing companies to build better cooling like closed-loop systems and heat reuse, where some centers even send leftover heat to warm nearby homes. And to deal with public pressure and cost, many tech companies are now paying for new solar, wind, and nuclear power, which helps clean energy grow faster.
But the bad sides are big too. **AI's water use could hit 4.2–6.6 billion cubic meters a year by 2027 — more than a whole country like Denmark uses.** These centers are often built where water is already scarce, so they compete with local people and farmers for it. This has already led to calls to stop building more in parts of the American West. Power use is a problem too; **data centers could use about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030**, almost three times what Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Nigeria use together. In Ireland, they already use over a fifth of all metered power. If that power isn't clean, it means more CO₂, even when companies claim to be "carbon neutral." **These centers could also take up over 14,500 square kilometers of land by 2030  twice the size of Jakarta, often pushing out nature. And many companies don't share clear numbers on their water and power use, making it hard to hold them accountable.**
In the end, AI is not going anywhere, and we don't want it to stop either. But the hidden cost is real: every chatbot answer uses water and electricity that most people never think about, and companies don't always share the full numbers. The solution is not to stop AI, but to make it more honest and efficient through better cooling systems and clearer reporting. If we recognise this cost now, we can enjoy AI without the planet paying the price for it.
