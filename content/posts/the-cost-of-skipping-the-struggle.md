It is tempting to jump straight from a question to a polished answer. The less comfortable step in between — trying, getting stuck, and working out why — can feel like wasted time.

But if we routinely ask a tool to do that work, a difficult question follows: **which parts of the thinking are we still practising?**

## What the evidence does and does not say

A 2025 study by researchers at Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon University examined knowledge workers’ self-reported use of generative AI and critical thinking. It found an association between greater confidence in generative AI and less reported critical-thinking effort.

That is worth taking seriously. It is also important to be precise: a survey of reported effort does **not** establish that using AI causes permanent cognitive decline. It does not measure everyone’s experience, either.

## The risk we want to examine

Our concern is a habit: accepting an answer before forming a view of our own. When an explanation sounds coherent, it can be easy to skip checking its assumptions or considering a different argument.

This is a question about how we use a tool, as well as what the tool can do. A summary can become the start of a closer reading, or a substitute for reading at all.

> A useful test: can I explain why I agree, without repeating the answer I was given?

## Leave room for your own attempt

Here are three practices to experiment with:

1. **Think first.** Write your initial answer or outline before requesting one.
2. **Ask for a challenge.** Request counterarguments, then decide whether they hold up.
3. **Close the tool.** Explain the idea in your own words and notice what you cannot yet explain.

These are suggested habits, not proven protection against every cognitive risk. Their purpose is to preserve an active role in the work.

The question we will keep returning to is simple: does this use of AI give us something to think with, or an excuse to stop thinking?
