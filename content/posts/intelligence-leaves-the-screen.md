An AI answer arrives as text on a screen. Behind it sits something much more tangible: computing hardware and the infrastructure that keeps it running.

This is the physical implementation of AI we want to explore — from individual chips to entire data centres.

## Start with the chip

Servers can contain both general-purpose processors, or CPUs, and specialised accelerators such as graphics processing units, or GPUs. The International Energy Agency describes these as part of the equipment supporting AI training and deployment in data centres.

But a processor is one component of a larger system. To understand the hardware, we also need to look at memory, storage, and the connections between machines.

## Zoom out to the data centre

A data centre houses racks of servers alongside storage and networking equipment. Cooling systems help maintain operating conditions, while backup power supports reliability during outages.

That gives us several layers to investigate:

- **Chips and servers:** where computation happens.
- **Storage and networks:** where data is kept and how it moves.
- **Cooling and power:** what keeps the equipment operating.

## Look at the whole system

Our aim is to make that infrastructure easier to understand. When a new AI chip or facility is announced, we want to ask what it does, how it connects to the rest of the system, and which practical constraints it addresses.

> The interface is what we see. The infrastructure is what makes it possible.
